## Player Backstory — "To The Ocean"

**Who they were:**
A crew-boat greenhorn — a deckhand on a working fishing vessel, a few weeks into the job. Not a captain, not a specialist. They'd seen the crew run nets, traps, and pots, handed off ropes and gear, but never had to run the operation themselves. Boat-savvy by osmosis, not training.

**The inciting event:**
Their ship is attacked and sunk by pirates. In the chaos, they make it off the sinking vessel and into the ship's lifeboat — a pontoon-style rescue craft, standard safety equipment carried aboard for exactly this kind of emergency. It's not built for fishing, hauling, or long voyages. It's built to keep one person alive long enough to be found. The pirates are gone, but the boat is damaged and the radio is dead. Nobody is coming to rescue you, you're on your own.

**Where the player starts:**
Alone, adrift, in a boat meant to be a stopgap — now their only home. Wide and stable, low to the water, built for survival rather than capability. Everything from here is scavenged, improvised, or learned the hard way.

**Why this works mechanically:**
- Basic boat operation is assumed knowledge — no need for a deep sailing tutorial.
- Specialized fishing gear (pots, traps, lines) is *unfamiliar* — natural excuse to gate and tutorialize gear introduction over time.
- The pontoon lifeboat justifies early-game limitations: low storage, low speed, low freeboard — vulnerability is baked into the starting equipment, not just a difficulty setting.
- Boat progression (repair, salvage, upgrade, eventual replacement) becomes a narrative payoff, not just a stat unlock — moving up from "the boat that saved my life" to something built for this world.
